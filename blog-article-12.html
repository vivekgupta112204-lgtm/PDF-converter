The History of the PDF Format

By PDF Converter Team • 8 min read

It is hard to imagine a world without PDFs. But in the early 1990s, sharing documents between computers was a nightmare. A file created on a Windows PC would look like gibberish on a Mac, and printing it correctly was a gamble.

Enter Dr. John Warnock, co-founder of Adobe, and his vision for "The Camelot Project".

1991: The Camelot Project

Warnock wrote a white paper titled "The Camelot Project". His goal was simple but revolutionary: to create a file format that could capture a document from any application and send it anywhere, to be viewed and printed on any machine.

"Imagine if you could send full text and graphics documents (newspapers, magazine articles, technical manuals etc.) over electronic mail networks. These documents could be viewed on any machine and any selected document could be printed locally. This capability would truly change the way information is managed." — John Warnock

1993: PDF 1.0 Launches

Adobe officially launched PDF 1.0 at the Comdex conference. However, it wasn't an instant hit.

  • The software to create PDFs (Adobe Acrobat) was expensive.
  • Even the Reader software cost money!
  • The files were large and slow to download on dial-up internet.

The Turning Point: Acrobat Reader Becomes Free

Adobe made a strategic decision that changed history. They decided to distribute Adobe Acrobat Reader for free. This removed the barrier to entry. Anyone could view a PDF, meaning businesses and governments could start using it for forms (like the IRS tax forms) knowing users could actually open them.

2008: An Open Standard

For years, PDF was proprietary to Adobe. In 2008, Adobe released PDF as an open standard (ISO 32000-1). This meant any developer could create software to make, read, or edit PDFs without paying royalties to Adobe.

This explosion of third-party tools is why websites like ours can exist today!

The Future of PDF

Today, PDF continues to evolve. We have PDF/A for long-term archiving, PDF/X for printing, and PDF/E for engineering. It remains the gold standard for digital paper—reliable, secure, and universal.
